Why locals call it one of Seoul’s three walkable rail paths

Hangdong Railroad (항동철길) runs through Guro-gu, Seoul, threading past low houses and green margins before it brushes Pureun Arboretum (푸른수목원). Visit Seoul pitches it as a simple, photogenic promenade: rails underfoot, trees overhead, and an easy half-day with the city’s first municipal arboretum.

VISITKOREA’s Korean portal (대한민국 구석구석) folds the tracks into its Pureun Arboretum feature: cosmos along the old line in autumn, kids laughing on the sleepers, elders pausing in memory. An 열린관광 open-tourism itinerary pairs Pureun Arboretum with 항동기찻길, noting an entrance ramp and accessibility features shared with the garden.

Seoul media and local coverage often group Hangdong with Gyeongui Line Forest Park and Gyeongchun Line Forest Park as the city’s three walkable rail corridors—the industrial cousin that kept its steel instead of becoming a paved linear park.

The line’s story starts in May 1959, when KG Chemical (경기화학) built a freight spur to move fertilizer raw materials and products. Guides describe about 4.5 km from Oryu-dong (Seoul) toward Okgil-dong (Bucheon) on the old Oryu Line (오류선 / 경기화학선). Trains once ran frequently past coal and steel works; today most of the corridor is a walkway. Local reports still note occasional military freight—sometimes described as rare, sometimes as a slow early-morning move—so treat the rails as a living railway, not a closed museum set.

What visitors actually walk is shorter and greener: roughly 1–2 km of the photogenic stretch near Hangdong and the arboretum, with sculptures and life-stage phrases on some ties—work celebrated since Sungkonghoe University students helped dress the corridor (Korea Herald).

What to do on the tracks

  • Walk the sleepers: gravel, weeds, and rusty rail in a residential–green belt—Seoul without the skyline squeeze
  • Photo stops: the faux “Hangdong Railroad Station” mood zone, student sculptures, and short phrases cut into wooden ties
  • Slide into Pureun Arboretum: Seoul’s first city-run arboretum sits beside the line—themed gardens, Hangdong Reservoir reeds, rose beds, and a forest education center
  • Link to Guro Ollegil: trail markers connect toward Cheonwangsan and forest loops if you want more than rails

Bring water and sun cover. There is little shade and no shop on the tracks; pack out trash.

Pureun Arboretum—natural next step after the rails. Photo: Shinfull / Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA 4.0

How to visit (practical)

  • Address: 1189 Ori-ro, Guro-gu, Seoul (서울특별시 구로구 오리로 1189)
  • Hours: outdoor path; open year-round (pair with arboretum hours)
  • Fee: free to walk the railroad
  • Pureun Arboretum: 240 Yeondong-ro, Guro-gu; typically free entry; inquire +82-2-2686-3200
  • Local inquiry: +82-2-860-2278 (Guro culture desk, open-tourism listings)
  • Official English: Visit Seoul — Hangdong Railroad

Getting there (rough guide)

  • Subway Line 7, Cheonwang Station, Exit 3 — about a 5-minute walk to the tracks
  • Alternatives: Cheonwang Exit 2 or Oryu-dong Station (Line 1)—confirm locally
  • For the arboretum first: Onsu Station (Lines 1 & 7) + Guro 07 village bus to Pureun Arboretum
  • Parking: no dedicated railroad lot; use Pureun Arboretum parking when available (weekends fill early)

English help nationwide: 1330 Korea Travel Hotline.

Cheonwang Station Exit 3—the usual subway approach. Photo: LERK / Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA 4.0

When it works best

  • Spring and autumn: green barley, cosmos, and soft light along the corridor
  • Weekday mornings: quieter than weekend family crowds
  • Arboretum combo days: rails + Pureun gardens + reservoir decks
  • Accessibility-aware visitors: use the entrance ramp noted by 열린관광; rails and ballast remain uneven

Safety note: this is not a fully abandoned line. Stay clear of any approaching freight, keep children close, and never assume the track is “dead.”

A traveler’s takeaway

Hangdong is industrial Seoul that never fully left: fertilizer cars, then neighborhood shortcuts, then a gentle tourist walk that still shares space with rare freight. For English-language travelers, it is the easiest way to feel Guro’s green edge—rails, poems on the ties, and an arboretum door a few steps away—without leaving the subway map.
